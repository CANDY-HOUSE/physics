Part I · Special RelativityChapter 1　Special Relativity

1.1　Geometrizing Light, Geometrizing the World

When you watch a YouTube video, the YouTuber lives in a two-dimensional plane; add a playback timeline, and it becomes a three-dimensional spacetime. This is a flip book: many two-dimensional photos stacked exactly on top of one another, each differing only slightly from the last; flip through them quickly and they become a video. The so-called time axis is just the page number; perhaps time does not exist at all.

Light is in the book too: with each page flipped, light advances a fixed distance. The speed of light \(c\) is just the conversion constant between "distance in the plane" and "page number"—the page number times \(c\) is the distance. This is the geometrization of light. With each page flipped, nothing can travel farther than this. This upper limit is like a parameter someone hard-coded—perhaps the world is virtual, and the parameter was hard-coded for fear that the world would spin out of control and crash.

Our three-dimensional space is actually a pop-up flip book: each page is not a photo but an entire three-dimensional world. This is the geometrization of the world.

Einstein proposed two postulates:

Postulates

Principle of relativity: the laws of physics take the same form in all inertial frames (observers that do not accelerate).

Constancy of the speed of light: the speed of light in vacuum \(c\) is the same for all inertial observers, independent of the motion of the source or the observer.

The second postulate is an experimental fact (the Michelson–Morley experiment detects no difference in the speed of light in any direction). The second postulate contradicts the everyday intuition of "adding velocities," so our notions of time and space must be revised. Below, we work out one by one what they must be revised into.

1.2　Time Dilation: Light Fired at the Ceiling of the Train

A light source on the floor of a train fires light straight up at a mirror on the ceiling, and the light reflects back to the floor. The height from the floor to the mirror is \(h\).

Train frame: the light goes straight up and straight down; the round trip takes \(\dfrac{2h}{c}\).

Ground frame: during the light's round trip the train is moving, so the light travels along slanted lines, and the total path is longer than \(2h\). The speed of light is still \(c\); the path is longer, so the trip takes longer than \(\dfrac{2h}{c}\).

Now compute exactly how long the trip takes. From the figure above, the time for the light to climb \(h\) is:

$$ \Delta{}^{\text{G}}t = \frac{h}{\sqrt{c^2-v^2}} = \frac{h}{c}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2}} $$

When a time \(\dfrac{h}{c}\) passes on the train, the time that passes on the ground is \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2}}\) times as long. This factor will keep appearing from now on, and we denote it by:

$$ \boxed{\;\gamma \equiv \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2}} \;>\; 1\;} $$

For the same phenomenon, the time measured in the ground frame is \(\gamma\) times as long as in the train frame—moving clocks run slow.

1.3　Length Contraction

A train sweeps at \(v\) past two points on the ground separated by \({}^{\text{G}}L\). Velocity = length / time, and the magnitude of this relative velocity must be the same whether measured on the train or on the ground:

$$ v = \frac{{}^{\text{G}}L}{\Delta{}^{\text{G}}t} = \frac{{}^{\text{T}}L}{\Delta{}^{\text{T}}t} $$

The two events "passing point A" and "passing point B" both happen at the same place, the front of the train; the train's clock is exactly the clock that runs slow in Section 1.2: \(\Delta{}^{\text{T}}t = \dfrac{\Delta{}^{\text{G}}t}{\gamma}\). The denominator gets smaller, so the numerator must get smaller along with the denominator for the ratio to stay unchanged:

$$ \boxed{\;{}^{\text{T}}L = \frac{{}^{\text{G}}L}{\gamma} = \sqrt{1-\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2}\; {}^{\text{G}}L\;} $$

When a moving observer measures a length on the ground, the length shrinks by the factor \(\dfrac{1}{\gamma}\); by the principle of relativity, the moving train measured from the ground is just as short (along the direction of motion; the perpendicular direction is unchanged). Combining 1.2 and 1.3: With a relative velocity, you see the other party in slow motion, and you see the other party squashed flat.

1.4　Lorentz Transformation

An event has coordinates \(({}^{1}x,\ {}^{1}t)\) in frame 1 and coordinates \(({}^{2}x,\ {}^{2}t)\) in frame 2 (moving forward at \(v\)). We assemble the conversion formulas directly from the conclusions of 1.3 and 1.2.

First equation (length contraction): at time \({}^{2}t\) in frame 2, the ground origin is located at \(-v\cdot{}^{2}t\), so the distance from the event to the ground origin, measured with frame 2's ruler, is \({}^{2}x + v\cdot{}^{2}t\). But the length of this distance on the ground is \({}^{1}x\), and frame 2 sees lengths on the ground shrink by the factor \(\dfrac{1}{\gamma}\) (Section 1.3):

$$ {}^{2}x + v\cdot{}^{2}t = \frac{{}^{1}x}{\gamma} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad {}^{1}x = \gamma\,({}^{2}x + v\cdot{}^{2}t) $$

Second equation (symmetry): multiply time by \(c\) and write \(c\cdot{}^{1}t\); the unit is then distance— time and position are the same kind of quantity, and \(c\) is only a conversion constant. The first equation then reads

$$ {}^{1}x = \gamma\left({}^{2}x + \tfrac{v}{c}\cdot c\cdot{}^{2}t\right) $$

The constancy of the speed of light says: \({}^{1}x = c\cdot{}^{1}t\) and \({}^{2}x = c\cdot{}^{2}t\) are the same ray of light—\({}^{1}x\) and \(c\cdot{}^{1}t\) play completely symmetric roles. Swap \({}^{1}x \leftrightarrow c\cdot{}^{1}t\) and \({}^{2}x \leftrightarrow c\cdot{}^{2}t\) in the first equation; the equation must still hold, which directly gives:

$$ c\cdot{}^{1}t = \gamma\left(c\cdot{}^{2}t + \tfrac{v}{c}\cdot {}^{2}x\right) $$

(Check: substitute \({}^{2}x = c\cdot{}^{2}t\); the right-hand sides of the two equations are equal, giving \({}^{1}x = c\cdot{}^{1}t\)—light moves at the speed of light in both frames.)

Divide the second equation by \(c\) to recover \({}^{1}t\), and rearrange:

$$ \boxed{\;{}^{1}x = \gamma\,({}^{2}x + v\cdot{}^{2}t), \qquad {}^{1}t = \gamma\left({}^{2}t + \frac{v}{c^2}\cdot{}^{2}x\right)\;} $$

This is the Lorentz Transformation. The inverse transformation replaces \(v\) with \(-v\):

$$ {}^{2}x = \gamma\,({}^{1}x - v\cdot{}^{1}t), \qquad {}^{2}t = \gamma\left({}^{1}t - \frac{v}{c^2}\cdot{}^{1}x\right) $$

The time dilation of 1.2 is a special case: take \({}^{2}x = 0\) to get \({}^{1}t = \gamma\cdot{}^{2}t\). When \(v \ll c\), \(\gamma \to 1\) and \(\frac{v}{c^2}\cdot{}^{2}x \to 0\), and we fall back to the Galilean transformation \({}^{1}x = {}^{2}x + v\cdot{}^{1}t\), \({}^{1}t = {}^{2}t\).

The Lorentz Transformation can also be expressed with matrices:

$$ \begin{pmatrix}c\cdot{}^{1}t\\[4pt] {}^{1}x\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\gamma & \gamma\,\dfrac{v}{c}\\[8pt] \gamma\,\dfrac{v}{c} & \gamma\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c\cdot{}^{2}t\\[4pt] {}^{2}x\end{pmatrix} $$

Inverse transformation:

$$ \begin{pmatrix}c\cdot{}^{2}t\\[4pt] {}^{2}x\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\gamma & -\gamma\,\dfrac{v}{c}\\[8pt] -\gamma\,\dfrac{v}{c} & \gamma\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c\cdot{}^{1}t\\[4pt] {}^{1}x\end{pmatrix} $$

Check: multiplying the two matrices gives the identity matrix (1.2: \(\gamma = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2}}\), so \(\gamma^2\Big(1-\left(\dfrac{v}{c}\right)^2\Big) = 1\)):

$$ \begin{aligned} &\begin{pmatrix}\gamma & \gamma\,\dfrac{v}{c}\\[8pt] \gamma\,\dfrac{v}{c} & \gamma\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma & -\gamma\,\dfrac{v}{c}\\[8pt] -\gamma\,\dfrac{v}{c} & \gamma\end{pmatrix}\\[14pt] &= \begin{pmatrix}\gamma^2 - \gamma^2\left(\dfrac{v}{c}\right)^2 & -\gamma^2\,\dfrac{v}{c} + \gamma^2\,\dfrac{v}{c}\\[8pt] \gamma^2\,\dfrac{v}{c} - \gamma^2\,\dfrac{v}{c} & -\gamma^2\left(\dfrac{v}{c}\right)^2 + \gamma^2\end{pmatrix}\\[14pt] &= \begin{pmatrix}\gamma^2\Big(1-\left(\dfrac{v}{c}\right)^2\Big) & 0\\[8pt] 0 & \gamma^2\Big(1-\left(\dfrac{v}{c}\right)^2\Big)\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} $$

1.5　A New Velocity Addition Rule

An object starts from the origin and moves at constant velocity. Frame 1 measures the velocity \({}^{1}v = \dfrac{{}^{1}x}{{}^{1}t}\), frame 2 measures \({}^{2}v = \dfrac{{}^{2}x}{{}^{2}t}\), and the velocity between the frames is \(v\). Substitute the Lorentz Transformation:

$$ {}^{1}v = \frac{{}^{1}x}{{}^{1}t} = \frac{\gamma({}^{2}x + v\cdot{}^{2}t)}{\gamma\left({}^{2}t + \frac{v}{c^2}\cdot{}^{2}x\right)} = \frac{\frac{{}^{2}x}{{}^{2}t} + v}{1 + \frac{v}{c^2}\frac{{}^{2}x}{{}^{2}t}} $$ $$ \boxed{\;{}^{1}v = \frac{{}^{2}v + v}{1 + \dfrac{{}^{2}v\,v}{c^2}}\;} $$

This equation replaces \({}^{1}v = {}^{2}v + v\). Check three cases:

Everyday speeds: when \({}^{2}v, v \ll c\), the denominator approaches 1, and we return to \({}^{1}v = {}^{2}v + v\). No wonder everyday life cannot detect the difference.

Light: substitute \({}^{2}v = c\):

$$ {}^{1}v = \frac{c + v}{1 + \frac{v}{c}} = \frac{(c+v)\,c}{c + v} = c $$

No matter how large \(v\) is, the result is \(c\)—the constancy of the speed of light is automatically guaranteed by the formula.

Stacking high speeds: on a train moving at \(0.9c\), throw a ball forward at \(0.9c\) (relative to the train). Seen from the ground:

$$ {}^{1}v = \frac{0.9c + 0.9c}{1 + 0.9^2} = \frac{1.8c}{1.81} \approx 0.994c $$

However you stack them, the result never exceeds \(c\).

1.6　Momentum Not Conserved?

A and B, each of mass \(m\), fly toward each other at speed \(v\). Treat A and B as one whole, with total mass \(M\); the center of mass C of the whole is a virtual point, not a physical object. By left–right symmetry, C is at rest.

Switch to the frame moving with B (B at rest); \({}^{B}v_{A}\) denotes the velocity of A measured in B's frame:

Before relativity, velocities simply added: \({}^{B}v_{A} = 2v\), \({}^{B}v_{C} = v\). The momentum of the whole equals the sum of the momenta of A and B; using \(p = mv\):

$$ m\,(2v) = M\,v \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad M = 2m $$

Adding momenta also yields, as a by-product, the addition of masses. But Section 1.5 has already shown that velocities cannot simply be added; the true velocities are

$$ {}^{B}v_{A} = \frac{2v}{1+\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2}\,, \qquad {}^{B}v_{C} = v $$

Substitute back: \(m\cdot{}^{B}v_{A} = Mv\). If \(M = 2m\), the left side is smaller than the right side, and the equality fails. We choose to keep "the momentum of the whole = the sum of the momenta of the parts"—this is only a human assumption; otherwise there is nothing to hold on to—so the definition \(p = mv\) simply has to be corrected.

1.7　Correcting Momentum

Let the corrected momentum be

$$ p = k(v)\, m v $$

\(k\) is a correction function shared by all objects, with \(k(0) = 1\). \(k\) has no units, so it can depend only on the unitless \(\frac{v}{c}\); when \(v \ll c\), \(k \approx 1\), and we return to Newton. The momentum-addition equation in the B frame becomes

$$ k({}^{B}v_{A})\, m\cdot{}^{B}v_{A} = k(v)\, M v \tag{1} $$

We still lack \(M\). Give the whole system an extra vertical velocity— switch to a frame moving downward at \(u\), and everything gains an upward \(u\):

The common drift \(u\) multiplies every vertical momentum by the same factor \(k(u)\); the rest is determined by the horizontal speed:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{A}&:\ k(u)\,k(v)\,mu\\ \text{B}&:\ k(u)\,k(v)\,mu\\ \text{C}&:\ k(u)\,k(0)\,Mu = k(u)\,Mu \end{aligned} $$

Adding the vertical momenta:

$$ k(u)\,k(v)\,mu + k(u)\,k(v)\,mu = k(u)\,Mu $$

Divide both sides by \(k(u)\,u\):

$$ 2\,k(v)\, m = M \tag{2} $$

Substitute \({}^{B}v_{A} = \dfrac{2v}{1+\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2}\) and \(M = 2k(v)m\) from (2) into (1):

$$ k({}^{B}v_{A})\; m\;\frac{2v}{1+\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2} \;=\; k(v)\cdot 2k(v)m \cdot v $$

Divide both sides by \(2mv\):

$$ \frac{k({}^{B}v_{A})}{1+\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2} = k(v)^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k({}^{B}v_{A}) = k(v)^2\left(1+\left(\tfrac{v}{c}\right)^2\right) \tag{3} $$

One more equation is needed. What the B frame sees is "A flies at \({}^{B}v_{A}\) toward B at rest, and the center of mass C moves forward at \(v\)"— play the same trick on this picture again: everything jointly gains a vertical \(u\), and the horizontal motion stays as before.

Adding the vertical momenta:

$$ k(u)\,k({}^{B}v_{A})\,mu + k(u)\,k(0)\,mu = k(u)\,k(v)\,Mu $$

Divide both sides by \(k(u)\,mu\), then use \(M = 2k(v)m\) from (2):

$$ k({}^{B}v_{A}) + 1 = k(v)\,\frac{M}{m} = 2k(v)^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k({}^{B}v_{A}) = 2k(v)^2 - 1 \tag{4} $$

Solve (3) and (4) together, eliminating \(k({}^{B}v_{A})\):

$$ 2k(v)^2 - 1 = k(v)^2\left(1+\left(\tfrac{v}{c}\right)^2\right) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k(v)^2\left(1-\left(\tfrac{v}{c}\right)^2\right) = 1 $$ $$ k(v) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2}} = \gamma \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;p = \gamma m v\;} $$

The correction function is, no more and no less, exactly the \(\gamma\) of Section 1.2. Incidentally, (2) says \(M = 2\gamma m > 2m\): the mass of the whole is larger than \(2m\)— momentum addition is preserved; mass addition is not.

1.8　Correcting Energy

The end of Section 1.7: \(M = 2\gamma m\); the mass of the whole does not equal the sum of the masses of the parts. What on earth is this \(\gamma m\)? Expand \(\gamma\) term by term and see:

$$ \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2}} = 1 + \tfrac12\left(\tfrac{v}{c}\right)^2 + \tfrac38\left(\tfrac{v}{c}\right)^4 + \tfrac{5}{16}\left(\tfrac{v}{c}\right)^6 + \cdots $$ $$ \gamma m = m + \frac{\tfrac12 m v^2}{c^2} + \frac{\tfrac38 m v^4}{c^4} + \cdots $$

The numerator of the second term is the Newtonian kinetic energy \(\tfrac12 mv^2\), yet the whole term is divided by \(c^2\)—odd. Then multiply both sides by \(c^2\) (everything is a human attempt; nothing is sacred):

$$ \gamma m c^2 = mc^2 + \tfrac12 m v^2 + \tfrac38\, \frac{m v^4}{c^2} + \cdots $$

Now it looks right: the second term is exactly the Newtonian kinetic energy, and the terms after the second term are high-speed corrections (small enough to neglect when \(v \ll c\)); the first term has nothing to do with motion—an object, merely by existing, carries \(mc^2\). So multiply both sides of \(M = 2\gamma m\) by \(c^2\), and guess that this might be energy:

$$ Mc^2 = \gamma m c^2 + \gamma m c^2 $$

"Kinetic energy + a fixed deposit"—humans currently guess that this quantity is energy:

$$ \boxed{\;E = \gamma m c^2\;} \qquad\qquad v = 0:\ \boxed{\;E_0 = mc^2\;} $$

1.9　Momentum, Energy, and Force

We have two equations in hand, both containing \(v\), which changes from frame to frame:

$$ E = \gamma m c^2, \qquad p = \gamma m v $$

Eliminate \(v\). Square and subtract, using \(\gamma^2\left(1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right) = 1\):

$$ E^2 - (pc)^2 = \gamma^2 m^2 c^4 - \gamma^2 m^2 v^2 c^2 = \gamma^2 m^2 c^4 \left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right) = (mc^2)^2 $$ $$ \boxed{\;E^2 - (pc)^2 = (mc^2)^2\;} $$

\(E\) and \(p\) each change from frame to frame, but this combination is the same difference in every frame—the square of \(mc^2\).

Divide instead, and what gets eliminated is the mass:

$$ \frac{E}{pc} = \frac{\gamma m c^2}{\gamma m v c} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;\frac{E}{pc} = \frac{c}{v}\;} $$

As the speed approaches the speed of light, \(\gamma\) becomes infinite, and \(\gamma m\) follows and becomes infinite too, yet \(m\) itself does not change, so \(m\) is very, very small compared with \(\gamma m\): \(\dfrac{mc^2}{E} = \dfrac{m\bcancel{c^2}}{\gamma m\bcancel{c^2}} = \dfrac{1}{\gamma} \to 0\). Divide both sides of the first equation by \(E^2\): \(1 - \left(\dfrac{pc}{E}\right)^2 = \left(\dfrac{mc^2}{E}\right)^2 \to 0\), so \(\dfrac{E}{pc} \to 1\), consistent with \(\dfrac{c}{v} \to 1\) from the second equation.

Low-speed check: take the square root of the first equation and expand term by term (\(\sqrt{1+x} = 1 + \tfrac12 x - \tfrac18 x^2 + \cdots\)):

$$ E = mc^2\sqrt{1+\dfrac{(pc)^2}{(mc^2)^2}} = mc^2 + \frac{p^2}{2m} - \frac{p^4}{8m^3c^2} + \cdots $$

The second term is exactly the Newtonian kinetic energy written in terms of momentum, \(\dfrac{p^2}{2m}\); the terms after the second term are high-speed corrections.

[Force]

What about force? In fact, we still cannot say clearly what force is; perhaps force does not exist, and all we know are changes in results. Which results, changing how?

$$ \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \gamma^2 c^2 - \gamma^2 v^2 = c^2 $$

Differentiate both sides:

$$ 2\gamma c^2\,d\gamma = 2(\gamma v)\,d(\gamma v) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad c^2\,d\gamma = v\,d(\gamma v) $$

Multiply both sides by \(m\):

$$ d(\gamma m c^2) = v\,d(\gamma m v) $$

Replace \(v\) with \(\dfrac{ds}{dt}\), where \(s\) is the distance traveled by the object:

$$ d(\gamma m c^2) = \frac{ds}{dt}\,d(\gamma m v) $$

Divide both sides by \(ds\) (\(v \neq 0\)):

$$ \boxed{\;\frac{d(\gamma m v)}{dt} = \frac{d(\gamma m c^2)}{ds}\;} $$

The change of momentum with respect to time equals the change of energy with respect to distance traveled; we call \(\dfrac{d(\gamma m v)}{dt}\) or \(\dfrac{d(\gamma m c^2)}{ds}\) force.

Then what does \(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{d(\gamma m v)}{dt}\,ds\) give? Conversely, what does \(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{d(\gamma m c^2)}{ds}\,dt\) give?

$$ \begin{aligned} \int_{\text{rest}}^{v}\frac{d(\gamma m v)}{dt}\,ds &= \int_{\text{rest}}^{v}\frac{d(\gamma m c^2)}{ds}\,ds = \int_{\text{rest}}^{v}d(\gamma m c^2) = \gamma m c^2 - mc^2\\[12pt] \int_{\text{rest}}^{v}\frac{d(\gamma m c^2)}{ds}\,dt &= \int_{\text{rest}}^{v}\frac{d(\gamma m v)}{dt}\,dt = \int_{\text{rest}}^{v}d(\gamma m v) = \gamma m v - 0 \end{aligned} $$

Integrating force over distance traveled gives the change in the energy of 1.8; integrating force over time gives the change in the momentum of 1.7:

$$ \int_{\text{rest}}^{v}\text{force}\,ds = \gamma m c^2 - mc^2 \qquad\qquad \int_{\text{rest}}^{v}\text{force}\,dt = \gamma m v - 0 $$

2.1　Review: The Doppler Effect

Sound propagates through air at \(v\). The source emits \(f_0\) wavefronts per second, and adjacent wavefronts are separated by

$$ \lambda = \frac{v}{f_0} $$

Source moving toward the observer at \(v_s\): adjacent wavefronts are emitted \(\dfrac{1}{f_0}\) apart, during which the earlier wavefront travels \(\dfrac{v}{f_0}\) and the source advances \(\dfrac{v_s}{f_0}\):

$$ \lambda_s = \frac{v}{f_0} - \frac{v_s}{f_0} = \frac{v - v_s}{f_0} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f = \frac{v}{\lambda_s} = \frac{v}{\dfrac{v - v_s}{f_0}} = f_0\,\frac{v}{v - v_s} $$

Observer moving toward the source at \(v_o\): the wavefront spacing is still \(\lambda\), but the wavefronts sweep past the observer at \(v + v_o\):

$$ f = \frac{v + v_o}{\lambda} = f_0\,\frac{v + v_o}{v} $$

Both at once: the observer sweeps past the compressed \(\lambda_s\) at \(v + v_o\):

$$ f = \frac{v + v_o}{\lambda_s} = f_0\,\frac{v + v_o}{v - v_s} $$

When moving apart, \(v_s \to -v_s\) and \(v_o \to -v_o\). Combined, the result is written as (upper signs for approaching, lower signs for moving apart):

$$ \boxed{\; f = f_0\,\frac{v \pm v_o}{v \mp v_s} \;} $$

2.2　An Accelerating Rocket: The Clocks at the Top and Bottom

A rocket accelerates upward with acceleration \(a\); the height from bottom to top is \(h\). The bottom sends a flash of light upward every \(T_{\text{bottom}}\), and the top receives one flash every \(T_{\text{top}}\). What is \(T_{\text{top}}\)?

[Method 1]

The rocket is accelerating, so the rocket is not an inertial frame—at the moment of emission, the rocket's velocity is \(v\). The light takes \(\Delta T\) to fly to the top: the light travels \(c\,\Delta T\), the top moves \(v\,\Delta T + \tfrac12 a\,\Delta T^2\), and the difference between the two is \(h\):

$$ h = c\,\Delta T - v\,\Delta T - \tfrac12 a\,\Delta T^2 $$

The last term is extremely small; drop the last term:

$$ (c - v)\,\Delta T = h \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta T = \frac{h}{c - v} $$

Does this equation hold in relativity? Didn't we say velocities cannot simply be added?—The addition rule of 1.5 governs changing frames: for the same object, frame 1 measures \({}^{1}v\) and frame 2 measures \({}^{2}v\); the rule says how to convert between the two. Here there is no change of frame: the position of the light \(c\,\Delta T\) and the position of the top \(h + v\,\Delta T + \tfrac12 a\,\Delta T^2\) are all coordinates measured in the same inertial frame with the same set of clocks and rulers; "the light catches up with the top" just means the two coordinates are equal, and subtracting coordinates is pure arithmetic. \(c - v\) is not the velocity of anything either, but the rate at which the gap shrinks—such a quantity can exceed \(c\) to begin with (when two beams of light head toward each other, the gap between them shrinks at \(2c\), perfectly legitimate).

During this time the top is being accelerated; when the top receives the light, the top's velocity is \(v + a\dfrac{h}{c - v}\).

Apply the Doppler formula—the source moves toward the observer at \(v\), and the observer moves away at \(v + a\dfrac{h}{c - v}\): \(f = f_0\,\dfrac{c - v_o}{c - v_s}\). Substitute \(v_s = v\), \(v_o = v + a\dfrac{h}{c - v}\), \(f_0 = \dfrac{1}{T_{\text{bottom}}}\), \(f = \dfrac{1}{T_{\text{top}}}\):

$$ \frac{1}{T_{\text{top}}} = \frac{1}{T_{\text{bottom}}}\,\frac{c - v - a\dfrac{h}{c - v}}{c - v} = \frac{1}{T_{\text{bottom}}}\left(1 - \frac{ah}{(c - v)^2}\right) $$

Expand term by term using \(\dfrac{1}{1-x} = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots\):

$$ \frac{ah}{(c - v)^2} = \frac{ah}{c^2}\cdot\frac{1}{\left(1 - \frac{v}{c}\right)^2} = \frac{ah}{c^2}\left(1 + \frac{v}{c} + \left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2 + \cdots\right)^2 = \frac{ah}{c^2} + 2\,\frac{ah}{c^2}\cdot\frac{v}{c} + \cdots $$

From the second term on, every term is \(\dfrac{ah}{c^2}\) multiplied by a power of \(\dfrac{v}{c}\)—the rocket is far too slow, so drop them all:

$$ \frac{1}{T_{\text{top}}} = \frac{1}{T_{\text{bottom}}}\left(1 - \frac{ah}{c^2}\right) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad T_{\text{top}} = \frac{T_{\text{bottom}}}{1 - \dfrac{ah}{c^2}} $$

Expand term by term using \(\dfrac{1}{1-x} = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots\), keeping the first-order term:

$$ T_{\text{top}} = T_{\text{bottom}}\left(1 + \frac{ah}{c^2} + \left(\frac{ah}{c^2}\right)^2 + \cdots\right) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\; \frac{T_{\text{top}} - T_{\text{bottom}}}{T_{\text{bottom}}} \approx \frac{ah}{c^2} \;} $$

[Method 2]

Without the Doppler formula, track the two flashes of light directly. The first flash takes \(\dfrac{h}{c - v}\) (as above); the second flash is emitted \(T_{\text{bottom}}\) later, when the rocket's velocity is already \(v + a\,T_{\text{bottom}}\), and takes \(\dfrac{h}{c - v - a\,T_{\text{bottom}}}\). The second flash sets out \(T_{\text{bottom}}\) later and, on top of that, takes the difference between the two flight times longer, so the interval received at the top is:

$$ T_{\text{top}} = T_{\text{bottom}} + \frac{h}{c - v - a\,T_{\text{bottom}}} - \frac{h}{c - v} = T_{\text{bottom}} + \frac{h\,(c - v) - h\,(c - v - a\,T_{\text{bottom}})}{(c - v - a\,T_{\text{bottom}})(c - v)} = T_{\text{bottom}} + \frac{a\,h\,T_{\text{bottom}}}{(c - v)(c - v - a\,T_{\text{bottom}})} $$ $$ \frac{T_{\text{top}} - T_{\text{bottom}}}{T_{\text{bottom}}} = \frac{ah}{(c - v)(c - v - a\,T_{\text{bottom}})} = \frac{ah}{c^2}\cdot\frac{1}{1 - \dfrac{v}{c}}\cdot\frac{1}{1 - \dfrac{v + a\,T_{\text{bottom}}}{c}} $$

Expand each of the two factors term by term using \(\dfrac{1}{1-x} = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots\):

$$ \frac{T_{\text{top}} - T_{\text{bottom}}}{T_{\text{bottom}}} = \frac{ah}{c^2}\left(1 + \frac{v}{c} + \left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2 + \cdots\right)\left(1 + \frac{v + a\,T_{\text{bottom}}}{c} + \left(\frac{v + a\,T_{\text{bottom}}}{c}\right)^2 + \cdots\right) = \frac{ah}{c^2} + \frac{ah}{c^2}\cdot\frac{v}{c} + \frac{ah}{c^2}\cdot\frac{v + a\,T_{\text{bottom}}}{c} + \cdots $$

From the second term on, every term is \(\dfrac{ah}{c^2}\) multiplied by the rocket's velocity divided by \(c\); drop them:

$$ \frac{T_{\text{top}} - T_{\text{bottom}}}{T_{\text{bottom}}} \approx \frac{ah}{c^2} $$

Same as Method 1.

\(T_{\text{top}} - T_{\text{bottom}} > 0\), so \(T_{\text{top}} > T_{\text{bottom}}\).

Up top it is already second 10—how is the bottom only at second 8? The light reflected off the bottom clock's hand at second 8 has only just arrived!

This differs from the "moving clocks run slow" of 1.2—there is no relative velocity between the two clocks—this is a new mechanism brought about by acceleration.

Where is the reference point for the comparison? That depends on where in the rocket the "starting point of measurement (the origin)" is placed.

1. The key lies in the "1" in the formula

$$ T_{\text{top}} = T_{\text{bottom}}\left(1 + \frac{ah}{c^2}\right) $$

The "1" is exactly the standard rate of time flow with no acceleration (\(a = 0\), a rocket at constant velocity); \(\dfrac{ah}{c^2}\) is the correction brought by the acceleration \(a\) and the height difference \(h\). The derivation takes the bottom of the rocket as the origin (\(h = 0\)), so the formula for the bottom is just

$$ T = T_{\text{std}} \times (1 + 0) = T_{\text{std}} $$

The bottom of an accelerating rocket and the bottom of a non-accelerating rocket have the same clock rate (density of film frames); at the top, \(h > 0\), the number of film frames increases (time runs faster).

2. What if the reference point is placed at the "center of the rocket" instead?

The origin can be chosen freely. The rocket is 100 meters tall; set the exact middle (at 50 meters) as the origin (\(h = 0\)): the clock rate at the center, counted in film frames, is the same as in a non-accelerating rocket (the same rate of time flow), the top (at 100 meters, \(h = 50\)) has more film frames (time runs faster), and the bottom (at 0 meters, \(h = -50\)) has fewer film frames (time runs slower). Where the reference origin is placed is called, in physics, the choice of the "local inertial frame."

2.3　Geometrizing Gravitation. A Human Guess: Acceleration = Gravity

When you stand on the ground, the soles of your feet are pushed up by the ground; when you stand in the rocket of 2.2 accelerating at \(a = g\), the soles of your feet are likewise pushed up by the floor. Shut inside the cabin, you cannot tell the two apart— so humans guess: acceleration and gravity are the same thing (the equivalence principle, Einstein 1907). This is only a guess; otherwise there is nothing to hold on to.

Replace the \(a\) of 2.2 with \(g\):

$$ \boxed{\; \frac{T_{\text{top}} - T_{\text{bottom}}}{T_{\text{bottom}}} \approx \frac{gh}{c^2} \;} $$

A clock higher up runs faster than a clock lower down—an experimental fact: in 1960, Pound and Rebka measured this difference in a 22.5 m tower using light emitted by Fe-57 nuclei, agreeing to within 10%; since then, from a satellite 10,000 kilometers up (1976, \(7\times10^{-5}\)) to 33 cm (2010) and an atomic cloud 1 mm tall (2022), in more than sixty years not a single measurement has deviated from \(\dfrac{gh}{c^2}\). A GPS satellite's clock, high up, runs 45 μs per day faster than clocks on the ground (the moving clock runs 7 μs slow, for a net gain of 38 μs); without correction, positioning would accumulate an error of ten kilometers per day.

[The Simplest Example of Geometrizing Gravitation: Light Bends Downward]

A beam of light travels horizontally, and the upper and lower ends of the wavefront differ in height by \(h\). Take the clock at the lower end as the standard: when 1 second passes at the lower end, \(1 + \dfrac{gh}{c^2}\) seconds have already passed at the upper end, \(\dfrac{gh}{c^2}\) seconds more. The speed of light measured at each end is \(c\), so in this 1 second the light at the upper end travels \(c\left(1 + \dfrac{gh}{c^2}\right)\), \(c\,\dfrac{gh}{c^2}\) more than at the lower end— the upper end of the wavefront runs farther and the lower end runs less far, so the whole wavefront turns downward. A light ray is perpendicular to the wavefront, so light bends downward.

Angular velocity of the wavefront's turning = the speed difference between the upper and lower ends divided by \(h\):

$$ \frac{c\,\dfrac{gh}{c^2}}{h} = \frac{g}{c} $$

The deflection is \(\dfrac{g}{c}\) per second. A stone thrown horizontally at speed \(c\) gains \(g\) of vertical velocity per second, and the stone's direction also turns by \(\dfrac{g}{c}\) per second—exactly the same. The derivation contains no "force" at all; using only the fact that time runs at different rates at different heights, light bends downward. Is gravitation perhaps not a force at all, but the shape of spacetime?—This is the guess of "geometrizing gravitation."

2.4　(Under Construction)

[Mass as Energy: Mass Without Mass]

Two beams of light, each of energy \(E\), fly toward each other from opposite directions, shut inside a massless box. The box as a whole: energy \(2E\), momentum \(\dfrac{E}{c} - \dfrac{E}{c} = 0\) (1.9: for light, \(E = pc\)). Substituting into 1.9's \(E^2 - (pc)^2 = (Mc^2)^2\):

$$ (2E)^2 - 0 = (Mc^2)^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad M = \frac{2E}{c^2} $$

There is nothing with mass inside, yet the box has mass—push the box and it resists; set the box on the ground and it has weight.

This is not idle speculation; it is a measured fact. The proton is made of quarks and gluons: gluons have no mass, and the rest masses of the three quarks together make up only 1% of the proton's mass; the remaining 99% is the kinetic energy and field energy shut inside (lattice QCD, 2018). The vast majority of the weight of your body is exactly this kind of mass without mass.

The reverse process can be measured too. When a proton and a neutron combine into a deuteron, a packet of light with energy 2.224 MeV is emitted, and the deuteron is lighter than the two added together by exactly that much. A 2005 experiment measured the energy from the wavelength of the light on one side, and measured the mass from the cyclotron frequency of ions in a magnetic field on the other, checking \(E = mc^2\) between the two sides; the discrepancy was less than 0.00004%.

[The Simplest Example of Geometrizing Potential Energy: Potential Energy Is How Fast Clocks Run]

Raise a stone of mass \(m\) by \(h\), and the stone's potential energy increases by \(mgh\). Written with the increase split apart:

$$ mgh = mc^2\cdot\frac{gh}{c^2} $$

\(mc^2\) is the rest energy of 1.8; \(\dfrac{gh}{c^2}\) is the result of 2.2: the number of seconds by which the clock at the top runs ahead of the clock at the bottom each second. The total energy of the stone up high:

$$ mc^2 + mgh = mc^2\left(1 + \frac{gh}{c^2}\right) $$

The quantity in parentheses is exactly the clock rate up high (taking the bottom clock as 1). The energy of a stone at rest = \(mc^2\) times the clock rate at the place where the stone is.

Light bending downward and a stone having potential energy use the same map of the time landscape. Is potential energy perhaps not in the stone at all, but rather how fast time runs where the stone is?—This is the guess of "geometrizing potential energy."

\(\dfrac{gh}{c^2}\) is the result of 2.2 after dropping the higher-order terms. Without dropping them, there are two exact versions.

A uniformly accelerating rocket: measure \(h\) with the rocket's own ruler, and take the acceleration measured at the bottom as \(g\); then the ratio of the top and bottom clocks is exactly \(1 + \dfrac{gh}{c^2}\), without dropping a single term. Four steps.

Step 1: Substitute the transformation of 1.4 into \((c\cdot{}^{1}t)^2 - {}^{1}x^2\):

$$ \begin{aligned} (c\cdot{}^{1}t)^2 - {}^{1}x^2 &= \gamma^2\left[\left(c\cdot{}^{2}t + \tfrac{v}{c}\cdot{}^{2}x\right)^2 - \left({}^{2}x + \tfrac{v}{c}\cdot c\cdot{}^{2}t\right)^2\right]\\ &= \gamma^2\left(1 - \tfrac{v^2}{c^2}\right)\left[(c\cdot{}^{2}t)^2 - {}^{2}x^2\right]\\ &= (c\cdot{}^{2}t)^2 - {}^{2}x^2 \end{aligned} $$

When frames are changed, this difference does not change.

Step 2: Take the worldline of the rocket's bottom to be the hyperbola \({}^{1}x^2 - (c\cdot{}^{1}t)^2 = \left(\dfrac{c^2}{g}\right)^2\). Differentiate both sides with respect to \({}^{1}t\):

$$ 2\cdot{}^{1}x\,\frac{d{}^{1}x}{d{}^{1}t} - 2c^2\cdot{}^{1}t = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad v = \frac{c^2\cdot{}^{1}t}{{}^{1}x} $$

Differentiate once more:

$$ \frac{dv}{d{}^{1}t} = \frac{c^2}{{}^{1}x} - \frac{c^2\cdot{}^{1}t\,v}{{}^{1}x^2} = \frac{c^2}{{}^{1}x^3}\left({}^{1}x^2 - c^2\cdot{}^{1}t^2\right) = \frac{c^2}{{}^{1}x^3}\cdot\frac{c^4}{g^2} $$

At \({}^{1}t = 0\), \({}^{1}x = \dfrac{c^2}{g}\) and \(v = 0\); substituting gives \(\dfrac{dv}{d{}^{1}t} = g\). By Step 1, this hyperbola is the same hyperbola seen from any inertial frame; switch to the frame moving at the same velocity as the rocket at some instant, and that instant is \({}^{2}t = 0\) of that frame, where the acceleration is \(g\). At every instant the rocket feels \(g\)—this is uniform acceleration.

Step 3: For the frame moving at the same velocity \(v\) as the rocket at some instant, the "same moment" of that frame is \({}^{2}t = 0\). By 1.4, \(c\cdot{}^{2}t = \gamma\left(c\cdot{}^{1}t - \tfrac{v}{c}\cdot{}^{1}x\right) = 0\), that is, \(c\cdot{}^{1}t = \tfrac{v}{c}\cdot{}^{1}x\): a straight line through the origin. The rocket's point, where \(v = \dfrac{c^2\cdot{}^{1}t}{{}^{1}x}\), lies exactly on this line. So the "line of simultaneity" at every instant of the rocket passes through the same point: the origin is the pivot, and the lines of simultaneity sweep around like the hand of a dial.

Step 4: The top of the rocket is \(h\) above the bottom (by the rocket's own ruler): the worldline of the top is another, concentric hyperbola \({}^{1}x^2 - (c\cdot{}^{1}t)^2 = \left(\dfrac{c^2}{g} + h\right)^2\). The top hyperbola is the bottom hyperbola scaled up about the origin by a factor of \(\dfrac{\frac{c^2}{g} + h}{\frac{c^2}{g}}\); the lines of simultaneity are straight lines through the origin, and after scaling they are still the same lines. The time the clock itself keeps: cut the worldline into small segments; in the same-velocity frame each segment has \(\Delta({}^{2}x) = 0\), so by Step 1, \((c\,\Delta({}^{2}t))^2 = (c\,\Delta({}^{1}t))^2 - (\Delta({}^{1}x))^2\); scale all coordinates up by some factor, and the clock time of each segment scales up by the same factor. So between two lines of simultaneity:

$$ \frac{T_{\text{top}}}{T_{\text{bottom}}} = \frac{\dfrac{c^2}{g} + h}{\dfrac{c^2}{g}} = 1 + \frac{gh}{c^2} $$

No approximation. Incidentally: the acceleration felt at the top is \(\dfrac{c^2}{\frac{c^2}{g} + h} = \dfrac{g}{1 + \frac{gh}{c^2}}\), smaller than at the bottom.

A real planet: Earth's \(g\) decreases with height, so a uniformly accelerating rocket can only model a small stretch near the surface. The clock rate outside an entire planet cannot be derived in this book—that requires solving Einstein's equations (Schwarzschild 1916); here the result is borrowed. At distance \(r\) from the center of a planet of mass \(M\), the rate of a clock at rest (taking a clock infinitely far away as 1):

$$ \sqrt{1 - \frac{2GM}{rc^2}} $$

\(G\) is Newton's gravitational constant, and \(\dfrac{GM}{r^2}\) is Newton's \(g\). The energy of a stone at rest is still \(mc^2\) times the clock rate:

$$ E = mc^2\sqrt{1 - \frac{2GM}{rc^2}} $$

Expanding term by term (\(\sqrt{1-x} = 1 - \tfrac12 x - \tfrac18 x^2 - \cdots\)):

$$ E = mc^2 - \frac{GMm}{r} - \frac{G^2M^2m}{2\,r^2c^2} - \cdots $$

The second term is Newton's potential energy \(-\dfrac{GMm}{r}\); from the third term on are corrections that Newton does not have. Comparing two heights:

$$ \frac{T_{\text{top}}}{T_{\text{bottom}}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \dfrac{2GM}{(r+h)\,c^2}}{1 - \dfrac{2GM}{rc^2}}} $$

Keep only the first-order term in \(h\), then drop \(\dfrac{2GM}{rc^2}\) (\(1.4\times10^{-9}\) at Earth's surface), and we return to \(1 + \dfrac{GMh}{r^2c^2} = 1 + \dfrac{gh}{c^2}\). The clock rate falls to zero at \(r = \dfrac{2GM}{c^2}\)—that is the boundary of a black hole.

[The Simplest Example of Geometrizing Energy: The Same Packet of Light Has Different Energies at Different Heights]

A stone sits at rest at height \(h\), with energy \(mc^2\left(1 + \dfrac{gh}{c^2}\right)\). Let go: when the stone has fallen to the bottom, its speed is \(v\) and its energy is \(\gamma mc^2\). Conservation of energy (a human assumption):

$$ \gamma mc^2 = mc^2\left(1 + \frac{gh}{c^2}\right) $$

At the bottom, turn the stone entirely into light (an electron and a positron that collide turn into light in just this way); the energy of the light is \(\gamma mc^2\). Fire the light toward the top, and at the top turn the light back into a stone at rest, which costs \(mc^2\). If the light still had energy \(\gamma mc^2\) on reaching the top, there would be a free profit of \(mgh\); let go again, do it all again—a perpetual motion machine. For there to be no free profit, the energy of the light after climbing to the top must be exactly \(mc^2\):

$$ \frac{E_{\text{top}}}{E_{\text{bottom}}} = \frac{mc^2}{\gamma mc^2} = \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{gh}{c^2}} = \frac{T_{\text{bottom}}}{T_{\text{top}}} $$

The same packet of light climbs upward and its energy decreases, by exactly the ratio of the bottom clock rate to the top clock rate, \(\dfrac{T_{\text{bottom}}}{T_{\text{top}}}\). Read in reverse: measured with the bottom clock, the energy of the packet of light at the top = the local energy of the packet at the top, \(E_{\text{top}}\), times the clock rate at the top, \(1 + \dfrac{gh}{c^2}\), which equals \(E_{\text{bottom}}\)—conserved. The stone of the previous subsection follows the same rule: local energy \(mc^2\), times the clock rate. Energy converts according to the clock rate: move the same thing to a place where clocks run faster, and, seen by the reference clock, the energy of the thing is greater—this is the guess of "geometrizing energy."

Experiment: this is exactly what Pound and Rebka measured in 1960—light emitted by Fe-57 nuclei climbed 22.5 m, and the energy of the light decreased by \(\dfrac{gh}{c^2} = 2.5\times10^{-15}\), in agreement to within 10%; in 1965 Pound and Snider got it to 1%. 2.2 says that the ticks received each second at the top also decrease by the same ratio: the energy and the frequency of light change in the same proportion—does this hint that the energy of light is proportional to the frequency?

[The Simplest Example of Geometrizing Kinetic Energy: Hypotenuse over Leg]

The light clock of 1.2: the ground frame sees the light travel along the hypotenuse, the train frame sees the light travel along the leg, and hypotenuse over leg is \(\dfrac{c}{\sqrt{c^2 - v^2}} = \gamma\). Taking the ground clock as the standard: while the clock on the train runs 1 second, \(\gamma\) seconds pass on the ground. The energy of 1.8:

$$ E = \gamma mc^2 = mc^2 \times \frac{\text{hyp}}{\text{leg}} $$

Kinetic energy is the extra part:

$$ E - mc^2 = mc^2\,(\gamma - 1) = mc^2 \times \frac{\text{hyp} - \text{leg}}{\text{leg}} $$

Kinetic energy = how much slower the object's own clock runs than the ground clock, times \(mc^2\). Potential energy is how much faster "the clock where the object is" runs than the reference; kinetic energy is how much slower "the object's own clock" runs than the clock where the object is; both are ratios of clocks. For a stone moving at speed \(v\) at height \(h\), multiply the two ratios:

$$ E = mc^2\left(1 + \frac{gh}{c^2}\right)\gamma $$

Experiment: for muons in the CERN storage ring in 1977, the momentum was fixed by the magnetic field to give \(\gamma = 29.3\); the measured lifetime was 29.3 times the lifetime at rest, in agreement to within two parts per thousand—the muon's own clock was 29.3 times slower, and the energy was 29.3 times the energy at rest.

[The Simplest Example of Geometrizing Mass: The Length of the Same Arrow]

1.9: \(E^2 - (pc)^2 = (mc^2)^2\), the same difference in every frame. Draw it: horizontal axis \(pc\), vertical axis \(E\); a particle is an arrow starting from the origin. Change frames, and the height (\(E\)) and the width (\(pc\)) of the arrow both change; the arrowhead slides along a hyperbola, and \(\text{height}^2 - \text{width}^2\) does not change—this is "length" in the geometry of spacetime: rotate a ruler, and the length of the ruler does not change (\((c\cdot{}^{1}t)^2 - {}^{1}x^2\) has the same form; see Step 1 of the uniformly accelerating rocket above).

Mass = the length of the arrow. Energy = the height of the arrow (the component in the time direction), momentum = the width of the arrow (the component in the space direction), kinetic energy = the amount \(E - mc^2\) by which the height of the arrow exceeds the arrow's own length. At rest the arrow stands upright, and the height is just the length \(mc^2\). A photon has \(m = 0\): an arrow of zero length, lying along the line \(E = pc\), with height and width but no length—1.9 says the photon is forced to run at the speed of light forever. Is mass, at bottom, simply a "length"?—This is the guess of "geometrizing mass."

[The Simplest Example of Geometrizing Mass: A Particle at Rest Is a Clock]

de Broglie's guess of 1924: inside a particle at rest there is something vibrating at frequency \(f\), and the energy of the vibration and the rest energy are one and the same:

$$ \text{Planck constant} \times f = mc^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad m = \frac{\text{Planck constant}}{c^2}\, f $$

Mass = how fast this clock runs, times a constant. For an electron \(f\) is \(1.2\times10^{20}\) Hz; for a cesium atom, \(3.0\times10^{25}\) Hz.

Experiment: in 2013 Müller's team built a clock whose ticks come directly from this \(f\) of the cesium atom—telling time with mass; the same apparatus, run in reverse, measures microscopic masses with a precision of \(4\times10^{-9}\). Since 2019, the kilogram is no longer that lump of metal in Paris, but is defined by Planck's constant (fixed at \(6.62607015\times10^{-34}\) J·s) together with \(c\) and the second; the SI Brochure inverts the definition into 1 kg = \(1.4755\times10^{40}\) × Planck's constant × cesium atomic clock frequency ÷ \(c^2\)—the definition of mass is a frequency times a constant. Is mass, at bottom, simply a frequency?—This, too, is the guess of "geometrizing mass."

[The Simplest Example of Geometrizing Mass: Mass Is the Slope of the Time Landscape]

As stated above, the clock rate increases by \(\dfrac{g}{c^2}\) for every meter of height; at Earth's surface, \(\dfrac{g}{c^2} = 1.1\times10^{-16}\) per meter. Turned around: without dropping a stone and without a scale, measuring the difference between clocks at two heights gives \(g\):

$$ g = c^2 \times \text{slope} $$

Newton: \(g = \dfrac{GM}{r^2}\), so

$$ M = \frac{c^2 r^2}{G} \times \text{slope} $$

Earth: slope \(1.1\times10^{-16}\) per meter, \(r = 6371\) km, giving \(M = 6.0\times10^{24}\) kg—weighing the Earth with two clocks. Here mass is not "how much stuff" but "how steep the surrounding time landscape is." How mass creates this landscape is the content of Einstein's equations, which this book cannot derive; here only Newton's \(\dfrac{1}{r^2}\) is borrowed.

Experiment: in 2010 NIST raised one of two ion clocks by 33 cm, and that was enough to measure the slope; in 2020 at the Tokyo Skytree, with two transportable optical lattice clocks, one on the ground and one on the observation deck at 450 m, a clock difference of \(4.9\times10^{-14}\) was measured, in agreement with \(\dfrac{gh}{c^2}\) to \(10^{-5}\); measuring height with clocks has become a new method of geodesy.

[The Simplest Example of Geometrizing Mass: Momentum in an Invisible Direction]

A guess (Kaluza 1921, Klein 1926): space has one more dimension, curled up into a small circle of circumference \(L\), too small to see.

A wave going once around the small circle must join back onto itself, so the circumference is an integer multiple of the wavelength:

$$ n\,\lambda = L \qquad (n = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \cdots) $$

Momentum is inversely proportional to wavelength (de Broglie: momentum = Planck's constant / wavelength), so the momentum along the small circle can only be an integer multiple of "Planck's constant / \(L\)"; \(n = 0\) is a wave that does not wind around.

A particle is at rest in our three-dimensional space, yet runs along the small circle: the particle's momentum \(p\) lies entirely in the direction we cannot see. Suppose the particle is massless in five dimensions; \(m = 0\) in 1.9 gives \(E = pc\). We cannot see \(p\); we see only something at rest with energy \(E\), and, following 1.8, read it as \(E = mc^2\):

$$ mc^2 = pc \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad m = \frac{p}{c} = \frac{n \cdot \text{Planck constant}}{L\,c} $$

"Rest mass" = momentum in the hidden dimension, determined entirely by geometry (\(L\)).

[Is Charge Geometry Too? Simplest Example: Momentum on the Same Small Circle]

Where does charge come from? The same picture: Kaluza–Klein says that the momentum running along the small circle is exactly what we call "charge"—it can only be an integer multiple of "Planck's constant / \(L\)", positive and negative correspond to the two directions of going around, and \(n = 0\) carries no charge. "Why are all charges integer multiples of the same unit, and why are there both positive and negative charges?" gets a geometric answer in this guess, and the same \(n\) gives both the charge and the mass at once. Using Klein's 1926 conversion, \(L\) is about \(10^{-32}\) m, seventeen orders of magnitude smaller than an atomic nucleus; substituting back into the mass formula of the previous subsection, a particle with \(n = 1\) has a mass of about \(10^{17}\) GeV, twenty orders of magnitude away from the electron's \(5\times10^{-4}\) GeV—this contradiction is the main reason Kaluza–Klein was shelved; modern string theory keeps the curled-up dimension, treats the electron as an \(n = 0\) mode, and the electron's mass comes from another source. Another geometric guess is Misner and Wheeler's "charge without charge" of 1957: electric field lines pass through a spacetime wormhole; seen from outside, one end looks like a positive charge and the other end looks like a negative charge, yet in fact there is no charge anywhere—but this kind of charge can take any value and cannot explain quantization, and Wheeler himself later abandoned the idea. Modern gauge theory regards charge as the winding number of a particle on an "internal small circle" (Weyl 1929, Wu–Yang 1975): also geometry, except that the circle is not in our spacetime.

Experiment: so far there is no evidence at all for extra dimensions—none from tabletop gravity experiments down to 30 μm, none from the LHC down below \(10^{-19}\) m; the charges of the proton and the electron are exactly opposite to within \(10^{-21}\) (relative); the Standard Model takes charge as an input and cannot explain why charge is quantized.

3.1　Where Magnetism, Light, and Electromagnetism Begin: Uniform Motion Flattens the Electric Field

In the \(q\) frame, the test charge \(q\) (mass \(m\)) is at rest at this instant (\({}^{q}t = 0\)), and the charge \(Q\) has always been moving at constant velocity \({}^{q}V\). The \(Q\) frame moves along with \(Q\).

$$ \begin{gathered} {}^{q}\gamma_{Q} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{{}^{q}V^2}{c^2}}}\\[6pt] {}^{Q}x_{Q} ={}^{q}\gamma_{Q}\cdot{}^{q}x_{Q},\qquad r_{\perp}\ \text{same in both frames}\ \text{(1.3)}\\[6pt] {}^{Q}r^2 = {}^{Q}x_{Q}^2 + r_{\perp}^2,\qquad {}^{q}r^2 = {}^{q}x_{Q}^2 + r_{\perp}^2 \end{gathered} $$

3.2　Magnetism Is Electricity! Where Ampère's law Comes From

This section uses only electrostatics and the relativity of Chapter 1 to calculate the magnetic force of a current-carrying wire on a moving charge (Purcell 1965).

[Method 1]

Ground frame: the wire contains two rows of charges: the positive charges have linear density \(\lambda\) and move right at \(u\); the negative charges have linear density \(-\lambda\) and move left at \(u\). The wire is uncharged, and the current is:

$$ I = 2\lambda u $$

At distance \(r\) from the wire, a charge \(q\) moves right at \(v\) (\(v < u\)). The wire is uncharged, so \(q\) feels no electric force.

\(q\) frame: moving right at \(v\) along with \(q\), so \(q\) is at rest at this instant. By the velocity addition of 1.5 (substituting \(-v\) for the velocity between the frames), the positive charges move right at \(u_+\) and the negative charges move left at \(u_-\):

$$ u_\pm = \frac{u \mp v}{1 \mp \dfrac{uv}{c^2}} $$

\(u_-\) is larger than \(u_+\). By 1.3, the faster charges run, the more densely the charges are packed; let the linear density of the charges at rest be \(\lambda_0\):

$$ \lambda = \gamma\,\lambda_0, \qquad \lambda_\pm = \pm\,\gamma_\pm\,\lambda_0 \qquad \left(\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{u^2}{c^2}}},\ \ \gamma_\pm = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{u_\pm^2}{c^2}}}\right) $$

Substituting \(u_\pm\):

$$ 1 - \frac{u_\pm^2}{c^2} = \frac{\left(1 \mp \frac{uv}{c^2}\right)^2 - \frac{(u \mp v)^2}{c^2}}{\left(1 \mp \frac{uv}{c^2}\right)^2} $$

After expanding the numerators, the two terms containing \(uv\) cancel each other:

$$ \begin{aligned} \left(1 \mp \tfrac{uv}{c^2}\right)^2 - \tfrac{(u \mp v)^2}{c^2} &= 1 \mp \tfrac{2uv}{c^2} + \tfrac{u^2v^2}{c^4} - \tfrac{u^2}{c^2} \pm \tfrac{2uv}{c^2} - \tfrac{v^2}{c^2}\\ &= 1 - \tfrac{u^2}{c^2} - \tfrac{v^2}{c^2} + \tfrac{u^2v^2}{c^4} = \left(1 - \tfrac{u^2}{c^2}\right)\left(1 - \tfrac{v^2}{c^2}\right) \end{aligned} $$

Taking the square root and the reciprocal:

$$ \gamma_\pm = \gamma\,\frac{1 \mp \dfrac{uv}{c^2}}{\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{v^2}{c^2}}} $$

Adding the two rows (\(\gamma\lambda_0 = \lambda\)):

$$ \begin{aligned} \lambda_{\text{net}} = \lambda_+ + \lambda_- = \lambda_0\,(\gamma_+ - \gamma_-) &= \gamma\lambda_0\,\frac{\left(1 - \frac{uv}{c^2}\right) - \left(1 + \frac{uv}{c^2}\right)}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}\\ &= -\frac{2\lambda u v}{c^2\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}} \end{aligned} $$

The wire that is uncharged in the ground frame is negatively charged in the \(q\) frame. The electric field of a long straight charged line is \(\dfrac{\lambda_{\text{net}}}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\) (Gauss's law), so \(q\), at rest, feels the electric force:

$$ {}^{q}F = q\,\frac{\lambda_{\text{net}}}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = -\frac{\lambda u}{\pi\varepsilon_0 c^2}\,\frac{qv}{r\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}} $$

The minus sign means attraction. Before switching back to the ground frame, first ask: could it be that this world has no such thing as "force" at all, and that force is really just change in momentum ÷ time?

$$ F = \frac{dp}{dt} $$

\(y\) is the coordinate axis perpendicular \(\perp\) to the wire. Consider the instant when \(q\) is at rest in the \(q\) frame:

$$ \begin{aligned} &\frac{d{}^{q}y}{d{}^{q}t} = 0 = \frac{d{}^{\text{G}}y}{d{}^{\text{G}}t} \;\Rightarrow\; {}^{q}\gamma = 1,\quad {}^{\text{G}}\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{v^2}{c^2}}}\\[4pt] &d{}^{\text{G}}t = {}^{\text{G}}\gamma\,d{}^{q}t\ \text{(1.2)},\quad d{}^{\text{G}}y = d{}^{q}y = dy\ \text{(1.3)}\\[4pt] &d{}^{q}p_{\perp} = d\Big({}^{q}\gamma m\,\frac{dy}{d{}^{q}t}\Big) = {}^{q}\gamma m\,d\frac{dy}{d{}^{q}t} + m\,\underbrace{\frac{dy}{d{}^{q}t}}_{0}\,d{}^{q}\gamma = m\,d\frac{dy}{d{}^{q}t}\\[4pt] &d{}^{\text{G}}p_{\perp} = d\Big({}^{\text{G}}\gamma m\,\frac{dy}{d{}^{\text{G}}t}\Big) = {}^{\text{G}}\gamma m\,d\frac{dy}{d{}^{\text{G}}t} + m\,\underbrace{\frac{dy}{d{}^{\text{G}}t}}_{0}\,d{}^{\text{G}}\gamma = {}^{\text{G}}\gamma m\,d\frac{dy}{d{}^{\text{G}}t}\\[4pt] &{}^{\text{G}}F = \frac{d{}^{\text{G}}p_{\perp}}{d{}^{\text{G}}t} = \frac{{}^{\text{G}}\gamma m\,d\dfrac{dy}{d{}^{\text{G}}t}}{{}^{\text{G}}\gamma\,d{}^{q}t} = \frac{m\,d\dfrac{dy}{{}^{\text{G}}\gamma\,d{}^{q}t}}{d{}^{q}t} = \frac{{}^{q}F}{{}^{\text{G}}\gamma} = -\frac{\lambda u}{\pi\varepsilon_0 c^2}\,\frac{qv}{r}\\[4pt] &I = 2\lambda u \;\Rightarrow\; {}^{\text{G}}F = -\frac{2\lambda u}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 c^2}\,\frac{qv}{r} = -\,qv\left(\frac{\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_0 c^2}\,I}{2\pi r}\right) \end{aligned} $$

The quantity in parentheses is exactly the magnetic field of a long straight wire; this is the Lorentz force (charge × velocity × magnetic field). In the \(q\) frame, the momentum of \(q\) is changed by the electric force; in the ground frame, the wire is uncharged, yet the momentum of \(q\) changes all the same, and we call this the magnetic force. Is the magnetic force perhaps not a force at all, but merely the length contraction (1.3) and time dilation (1.2) of spacetime?

[Method 2]

In a real metal wire, the positive ions do not move; only the free electrons move.

Ground frame: the positive charges are at rest, with linear density \(\lambda\); the negative charges have linear density \(-\lambda\) and move left at \(u\). The wire is uncharged, and the current is:

$$ I = \lambda u $$

The charge \(q\) again moves right at \(v\) at distance \(r\) from the wire.

\(q\) frame: running along with \(q\). The positive charges move left at \(v\), and by 1.3 become denser:

$$ \lambda_+ = \frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}} $$

The motion of the negative charges is the same as in Method 1, and the linear density of the negative charges at rest is \(\dfrac{\lambda}{\gamma}\); substituting the \(\gamma_-\) of Method 1:

$$ \lambda_- = -\,\gamma_-\,\frac{\lambda}{\gamma} = -\,\gamma\,\frac{1 + \frac{uv}{c^2}}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}\cdot\frac{\lambda}{\gamma} = -\,\frac{\lambda\left(1 + \frac{uv}{c^2}\right)}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}} $$

Adding:

$$ \lambda_{\text{net}} = \lambda_+ + \lambda_- = \frac{\lambda - \lambda\left(1 + \frac{uv}{c^2}\right)}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}} = -\,\frac{\lambda u v}{c^2\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}} = -\,\frac{I v}{c^2\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}} $$

The negative charges have an extra factor \(1 + \dfrac{uv}{c^2}\), so the wire is negatively charged. Written in terms of \(I\), the result is exactly the same as in Method 1 (\(I = 2\lambda u\)), and the following steps are also the same:

$$ {}^{q}F = q\,\frac{\lambda_{\text{net}}}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = -\,\frac{qIv}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 c^2\, r\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}} $$ $$ {}^{\text{G}}F = \frac{{}^{q}F}{{}^{\text{G}}\gamma} = -\,\frac{qIv}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 c^2\, r} = -\,qv\left(\frac{\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_0 c^2}\,I}{2\pi r}\right) $$

The magnetic force depends only on the current \(I\), and has nothing to do with which kind of charge is moving in the wire.

3.5　Gaussian Law, with No Electric Field Lines or Electric Flux

First of all, force does not exist. To explain \({}^{Q}r^2\), people two hundred years ago imagined that the influence of \(Q\), like the light from a light bulb, spreads out evenly in all directions. At distance \({}^{Q}r\), the influence is spread over an entire sphere; double the distance and the sphere becomes four times as large, so the same amount of influence is spread thinner, and each small patch gets only a quarter.

Faraday, in the nineteenth century, had no mathematical training. He sprinkled iron filings beside a magnet, saw the filings line up into curves, and called the curves "lines of force"; he also used the same lines to describe the electric field around charges, and he leaned toward believing that these lines really exist. Even earlier, Gauss (proved in 1813, general form published in 1840) had already worked out mathematically that the normal components of the force, added up over a closed surface, depend only on how much mass or charge is inside the surface; in the language of lines of force, the number of lines of force passing through a closed surface depends only on how much charge is inside. Later, Maxwell (1855–56) systematically wrote Faraday's lines of force as equations, and ten years later wrote down the complete set of electromagnetic equations (1865).

But electric field lines are also something humans thought up, and perhaps electric field lines do not exist at all; perhaps what really exists is the deformation of spacetime, which just happens to match these imaginary lines. It is like a map describing terrain with contour lines: the denser the contour lines, the steeper the slope; but walk up the mountain, and there is not a single white contour line on the ground. Although contour lines do not exist, contour lines are very convenient for counting slopes; so are electric field lines. Below, electric field lines are used to count out Gauss's statement.

Electric flux (\(Q\) frame): electric flux is the flux of electric field lines, that is, the number of electric field lines passing through a surface. The number of electric field lines passing through a unit perpendicular area is the magnitude of the electric field. If the outward direction of a small patch of area \(d{}^{Q}A\) makes an angle \(\alpha\) with \({}^{Q}\vec E\), the electric field lines passing through the patch are the same as those passing through the patch's projection \(\cos\alpha\cdot d{}^{Q}A\), so the number of lines is:

$$ d{}^{Q}\Phi_E \overset{\text{def.}}{=} {}^{Q}E\cdot\cos\alpha\cdot d{}^{Q}A = {}^{Q}\vec E\cdot d{}^{Q}\vec A $$

(\(d{}^{Q}\vec A\): the vector of magnitude \(d{}^{Q}A\) pointing outward.) Summing over the entire closed surface gives the electric flux through the closed surface, \({}^{Q}\Phi_E = \oint{}^{Q}\vec E\cdot d{}^{Q}\vec A\); exiting counts as positive, entering as negative.

Two definitions of the electric field: Definition 1 comes from physics (3.1): place a charge \(q\) at the point; the change of momentum over time per unit \(q\) is the electric field:

$$ {}^{Q}\vec E \overset{\text{definition 1}}{=} \frac{1}{q}\cdot\frac{d{}^{Q}\vec p_{q}}{d{}^{Q}t} $$

Definition 2 comes from the picture: at the point, take a small patch of area \(d{}^{Q}A_{\perp}\) perpendicular to the electric field lines; the number of electric field lines passing through the patch (that is, the electric flux \(d{}^{Q}\Phi_E\)) divided by that area is the magnitude of the electric field; the direction of the electric field is the direction of the electric field lines:

$$ {}^{Q}E \overset{\text{definition 2}}{=} \frac{d{}^{Q}\Phi_E}{d{}^{Q}A_{\perp}} $$

There is also a third formula. The third formula is not a definition but an observational result (Coulomb 1785): measured by Definition 1, the magnitude of the electric field at distance \({}^{Q}r\) from \(Q\) is:

$$ {}^{Q}E \overset{\text{observed}}{=} \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\cdot{}^{Q}r^2} $$

The two definitions describe the same electric field, so their values must agree. Take a sphere centered on \(Q\) with radius \({}^{Q}r\) for the comparison: the sphere is everywhere perpendicular to the electric field lines, and the \({}^{Q}\Phi_E\) electric field lines emitted by \(Q\) are spread evenly over the area \(4\pi\cdot{}^{Q}r^2\).

$$ \begin{gathered} {}^{Q}E = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\cdot{}^{Q}r^2}\ \text{(observation)}\\[6pt] {}^{Q}E = \frac{{}^{Q}\Phi_E}{4\pi\cdot{}^{Q}r^2}\ \text{(definition 2: number of lines ÷ area of the sphere)}\\[6pt] \frac{{}^{Q}\Phi_E}{\bcancel{4\pi\cdot{}^{Q}r^2}} = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0\cdot\bcancel{4\pi\cdot{}^{Q}r^2}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{}^{Q}\Phi_E = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} \end{gathered} $$

\({}^{Q}r\) cancels out: no matter how large the sphere, the number of electric field lines exiting is \(\dfrac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}\), which is exactly why, in the earlier figure, "the same 9 lines pass through every face." Therefore, as long as each charge \(Q\) is stipulated to emit \(\dfrac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}\) electric field lines, the two definitions give the same electric field.

Why \(\dfrac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}\) lines and not \(Q\) lines? Humans must have chosen the wrong units. \(\varepsilon_0\) is only the conversion constant between the unit of charge (coulombs) and the units of mechanics (newtons, meters); if charge is also measured in mechanical units instead (\(\varepsilon_0 = 1\); as in 3.4, Fraktur denotes quantities in such units), no \(\varepsilon_0\) appears in the result:

$$ \begin{gathered} \mathfrak Q = \frac{Q}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0}},\qquad \mathfrak q = \frac{q}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0}},\qquad {}^{Q}\vec{\mathfrak E} = \sqrt{\varepsilon_0}\cdot{}^{Q}\vec E\\[8pt] {}^{Q}\Phi_{\mathfrak E} = \oint{}^{Q}\vec{\mathfrak E}\cdot d{}^{Q}\vec A = \sqrt{\varepsilon_0}\cdot{}^{Q}\Phi_E = \sqrt{\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{Q}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0}} = \mathfrak Q\\[8pt] {}^{Q}\mathfrak E = \sqrt{\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\cdot{}^{Q}r^2} = \frac{Q}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0}\cdot 4\pi\cdot{}^{Q}r^2} = \frac{\mathfrak Q}{4\pi\cdot{}^{Q}r^2}\\[8pt] \frac{d{}^{Q}\vec p_{q}}{d{}^{Q}t} = q\cdot{}^{Q}\vec E = \frac{q}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0}}\cdot\sqrt{\varepsilon_0}\cdot{}^{Q}\vec E = \mathfrak q\cdot{}^{Q}\vec{\mathfrak E} \end{gathered} $$

In such units, the number of electric field lines emitted equals the amount of charge.

But if the surface is not a sphere centered on \(Q\), is the number of electric field lines exiting still the same? Yes.

Gauss's law (\(Q\) frame: \(Q\) at rest): cut a closed surface into many small patches, one of which is at distance \({}^{Q}r\) from \(Q\). Looking out from \(Q\), this small patch and a small patch on the sphere of radius 1 are cut out by exactly the same thin cone:

$$ d{}^{Q}\Phi_E = {}^{Q}E\cdot\cos\alpha\cdot d{}^{Q}A = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\cdot{}^{Q}r^2}\cdot\cos\alpha\cdot d{}^{Q}A = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\cos\alpha\cdot d{}^{Q}A}{{}^{Q}r^2}}_{\text{patch on the radius-1 sphere}} $$

When \(Q\) is inside the surface, the thin cones sweep all the way around and cut the radius-1 sphere exactly once, so the sum is the area of the radius-1 sphere, \(4\pi\) (a thin cone exits the surface one more time than the cone enters). When \(Q\) is outside the surface, each thin cone exits the surface as many times as the cone enters, and \(\cos\alpha\), negative once and positive once, cancels exactly:

$$ \oint d{}^{Q}\Phi_E = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot 4\pi = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}\ \text{(}Q\text{ inside)},\qquad \oint d{}^{Q}\Phi_E = 0\ \text{(}Q\text{ outside)} $$

Many charges at rest: the electric fluxes of the individual charges simply add; the charges outside the surface all contribute zero, leaving only the charge inside the surface, \(Q_{\text{in}}\):

$$ \oint\vec E\cdot d\vec A = \frac{Q_{\text{in}}}{\varepsilon_0}\ \text{(Gauss's law, one of Maxwell's equations)} $$

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{left and right faces: }&\ \big(E_x(x + dx) - E_x(x)\big)\cdot dy\cdot dz = \frac{\partial E_x}{\partial x}\,dx\cdot dy\cdot dz\\[6pt] \text{top and bottom faces: }&\ \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial y}\,dy\cdot dx\cdot dz,\qquad \text{front and back faces: }\ \frac{\partial E_z}{\partial z}\,dz\cdot dx\cdot dy \end{aligned} $$

(\(\dfrac{\partial E_x}{\partial x}\): the rate of change of \(E_x\) when only \(x\) varies while \(y\) and \(z\) stay fixed.) The six faces added together give the electric flux of the box; by Gauss's law, the electric flux of the box equals the charge in the box divided by \(\varepsilon_0\):

$$ \Big(\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial E_z}{\partial z}\Big)\,dx\cdot dy\cdot dz = \frac{\rho\,dx\cdot dy\cdot dz}{\varepsilon_0} $$

Divide both sides by \(dx\cdot dy\cdot dz\), and write the dot product of \(\nabla = \Big(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}, \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}, \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}\Big)\) and \(\vec E\) as \(\nabla\cdot\vec E\):

$$ \nabla\cdot\vec E = \frac{\partial E_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial E_z}{\partial z} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\ \text{(differential form of Gauss's law)} $$

3.6　Magnetic Field Lines and Magnetic Flux Do Not Exist

The Biot-Savart Law derived in 3.3: the magnetic field produced at distance \({}^{\text{G}}r\) by a short segment of current \({}^{\text{G}}I\,d{}^{\text{G}}\vec\ell\) is:

$$ d{}^{\text{G}}B = \frac{\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_0 c^2}\cdot{}^{\text{G}}I\,d{}^{\text{G}}\ell\,\sin{}^{\text{G}}\theta}{4\pi\cdot{}^{\text{G}}r^2}\ \text{(Biot-Savart Law, 3.3)} $$

Like Coulomb's electric field, this magnetic field is also inversely proportional to \({}^{\text{G}}r^2\). To explain \({}^{\text{G}}r^2\), the magnetic field, following the electric field, was designed as "the number of magnetic field lines passing through a unit perpendicular area," that is, magnetic flux ÷ area: double the distance and the area becomes four times as large, so the same number of magnetic field lines is spread thinner, and each small patch gets only a quarter.

Faraday called the curves traced out by iron filings beside a magnet "magnetic lines of force," and in 1852 argued that these lines really exist; Maxwell (1855–56) then put these lines into mathematics: thin tubes, each filled with an incompressible imaginary fluid. The "ring tubes" below are such thin tubes. However, this is only an ignorant scheme that Faraday and Maxwell devised to force an explanation, like the isobars on a weather map: there may be a pressure field, but there is not a single line in the sky.

As mentioned in 3.4, the magnetic force is in the end only a part of \({}^{\text{G}}E\), and the magnetic field may not exist at all; so magnetic field lines are imaginary lines drawn on an imaginary field.

$$ {}^{\text{G}}\vec B \overset{\text{3.4}}{=} \frac{{}^{\text{G}}\vec u\times{}^{\text{G}}\vec E}{c^2} \overset{\text{def.}}{=} \frac{d{}^{\text{G}}\Phi_B}{d{}^{\text{G}}A_{\perp}}\cdot{}^{\text{G}}\hat A_{\perp} $$

(\({}^{\text{G}}\hat A_{\perp}\): the unit normal vector of the perpendicular area \(d{}^{\text{G}}A_{\perp}\), pointing in the direction in which the magnetic field lines pass through.)

If there are many charges moving at constant velocity, compute each charge's \({}^{\text{G}}\vec B\) from that charge's own \({}^{\text{G}}\vec u\) and \({}^{\text{G}}\vec E\), then add them all up; charges at rest have no magnetic field.

Magnetic field lines are closed circles: a magnetic field line exits any closed surface (for example, the small box in the figure) as many times as the line enters; the same holds when the magnetic fields of many charges are added:

$$ \oint\vec B\cdot d\vec A = 0\ \text{(Gauss's law for magnetism, one of Maxwell's equations)} $$

Differential form: use the small box of 3.5, replacing \(\vec E\) with \(\vec B\); the magnetic flux of the box is zero:

$$ \begin{gathered} \Big(\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z}\Big)\,dx\cdot dy\cdot dz = 0\\[6pt] \nabla\cdot\vec B = \frac{\partial B_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z} = 0\ \text{(differential form of Gauss's law for magnetism)} \end{gathered} $$

At root, the reason is that the world has electric charges but no magnetic charges; at least so far, no magnetic monopole has been found. None means none! Magnetic field lines are not emitted from magnetic charges and have no starting point or end point; magnetic field lines are only imaginary loops: a line that enters a closed surface exits the closed surface; every entry has an exit, and the sum of entries and exits over an enclosing surface is always zero.

When many charges each move at constant velocity along straight lines in this plane, the equation above holds for each charge separately; the electric field and the magnetic flux both add up charge by charge, so the equation also holds for the total.

The magnetic field need not exist: the charges in the loop are at rest, and the only thing driving the charges is the electric field (3.1); the emf \(\oint{}^{\text{G}}\vec E\cdot d{}^{\text{G}}\vec\ell\) is computed entirely from the electric field. The magnetic field merely gives \(\dfrac{{}^{\text{G}}u^2}{c^2}\oint{}^{\text{G}}\vec E_{\perp}\cdot d{}^{\text{G}}\vec\ell_{\perp}\) a new name, writing that expression as \(-\dfrac{d{}^{\text{G}}\vec\Phi_B}{d{}^{\text{G}}t}\); from beginning to end, the magnetic field exerts no force at all.

A loop translating at constant velocity, a bent wire, a rotating loop, a rod sliding on rails: all of these can be projected and decomposed into combinations of this example, and the mathematical expressions in vector form are exactly the same. This example is the bottom-level logic of Faraday's Law.

In addition, the electric field of a single point charge is not uniform. Even if the point charge moves at constant velocity, for a fixed point in space the stretch of time during which the point charge passes by is a pulse; that is, the current there has changed. Countless pulses, each of a different size and spacing, can make up a transient or steady current of any waveform; so a time-varying current is likewise a combination of this example.

The differential form involves only the neighborhood of one point and is the most basic unit; integrating the differential form gives a result that applies to loops of any shape and to any group of charges moving at constant velocity.

3.9　Radiating Electromagnetic Waves

First, consider a \(Q\) that appears out of nowhere and stays at rest. Its electric field does not fill all of space immediately upon appearing; instead, the electric field is limited by the speed of light: starting from the position of \(Q\), the electric field is built outward at \(c\); after a time \(t\), only the region within radius \(c\,t\) has the electric field of \(Q\).

$$ E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{Q}{r^2}\ \text{(}r\text{: distance to }Q\text{; }r < c\,t\text{)} $$

Next, consider the direction of the electric field. On the two message fronts sent at times \(t_0\) and \(t_1\), take \(\vec P_0\) and \(\vec P_1\), respectively, along the same direction \(\hat r\) starting from \(Q\); the line joining the two points is the direction of the electric field at the two points \(\vec P_0\) and \(\vec P_1\) at this instant.

$$ \begin{aligned} &\vec P_0 - \vec P_1 = c\,(t - t_0)\,\hat r - c\,(t - t_1)\,\hat r = c\,(t_1 - t_0)\,\hat r\\[6pt] &\phantom{=\ }\text{(}Q\text{ at the origin; }t\text{: this instant; the radii of the two fronts are }c\,(t - t_0)\text{ and }c\,(t - t_1)\text{, respectively)} \end{aligned} $$

When \(Q\) is at rest, the line joining the two points runs along \(\hat r\) and, extended backward, passes through \(Q\): the electric field points along the radial direction, consistent with the Coulomb electric field in the equation above.

Next, consider \(Q\) moving forward at constant velocity \({}^{\text{G}}\vec u\). The message fronts sent at times \(t_0\) and \(t_1\) are centered at \(\vec x_Q(t_0)\) and \(\vec x_Q(t_1)\), respectively. Along the same direction \(\hat r\), take \(\vec P_0\) and \(\vec P_1\) on the two fronts, respectively; \(\vec P_0 - \vec P_1\) is the direction of the electric field at the two points \(\vec P_0\) and \(\vec P_1\) at this instant.

Wait! Why choose \(\hat r\) in the same direction as from \(Q(t_0)\) to \(\vec P_0\) to obtain \(\vec P_1\)?

Maybe \(Q\) is the real center of the universe, and it is the ground that is moving. Because the two have a relative velocity, the purple angle of \(\hat r\) looks different in the ground frame than in the \(Q\) frame (1.2); but even though the purple angles look different, the purple angles at every instant are still equal to one another, because in the \(Q\) frame, \(Q\) sends the messages that arrive at \(\vec P_0\) and \(\vec P_1\) in the same direction.

$$ \frac{{}^{q}E_{\perp}}{{}^{q}E_{/\mkern-5mu/}} = \frac{r_{\perp}}{-\,{}^{q}x_{Q}} $$

↑ Good heavens! Without using \(\gamma = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{v^2}{c^2}}}\) at all—a concept that makes no sense to junior high school students—and with nothing but junior high school geometry, we have obtained the same conclusion as in 3.1. No wonder that, seen as a whole, the electric field, though flattened, moves forward while keeping a fixed shape.

This is an electromagnetic wave. What? Some people say this is not an electromagnetic wave, that it only counts if there is acceleration? Fine, then let's change the originally immovable \(q\) to one held in place by a spring.

Some people say that only the change in the electric field passed on a second time by \(q\) is an "electromagnetic wave." Fine, I have nothing to say. In both cases, the input to the system, \(Q\), is no different, and the output in both cases is a periodic change of the electric field over time. As for whether acceleration is required for something to count as an "electromagnetic wave," I think that is merely a definition that whoever started it never thought through, one that does not stand up to scrutiny.

Take a \(Q\) at rest. If the frame the observer is in is accelerating, then tell me: does \(Q\) emit an electromagnetic wave or not? That is probably still debated to this day.

Above is the case of a \(Q\) moving at constant velocity that suddenly stops. Some people say that only the kink in the electric field lines caused by this kind of change in velocity deserves to be called an electromagnetic wave. Fine, then let's discuss this case.
Actually, the dashed parts should not be connected, because \(Q\) stops instantly and the speed of light cannot keep up. This is just like the Heaviside step function: people always avoid discussing whether the Heaviside step function is actually continuous at the jump.

But in fact, we have also never yet encountered a case of stopping instantly, with infinite acceleration, so that the speed of light cannot keep up. Below is the case of \(Q\) decelerating with uniform acceleration until coming to a complete stop.

Let \(Q\) go back and forth a few more times, and the periodic change in the electric field that we see is what people call an electromagnetic wave; and the back-and-forth motion of \(Q\) is what those people call "radiation." There is actually nothing profound in meaning or content here.

How do we analyze the electric field built by an accelerating \(Q\)?

In the \(Q\) frame, the two messages have the same direction (\({}^{Q}\theta_0 = {}^{Q}\theta_1\)); but the velocities of \(Q\) at \(t_0\) and \(t_1\) differ. Seen from the ground, the vertical direction does not stretch or shrink, while the degree of stretching or shrinking in the horizontal direction varies with the velocity of \(Q\) (1.3), so the directions \({}^{q}\theta_0\) and \({}^{q}\theta_1\) of the two \(\overrightarrow{QP}\) also differ.

$$ \begin{aligned} &d\vec P = \vec P_0 - \vec P_1 = \underbrace{c\,dt\;\hat r_0 - \big(v\,dt + {\color[RGB]{252,98,85}\enclose{updiagonalstrike}{\style{color:var(--fg)}{\tfrac12\,a\,dt^2}}}\big)\,\hat x}_{\text{same as 3.9}} + \underbrace{c\,(t-t_1)\,(\hat r_0 - \hat r_1)}_{\text{arc along the front}}\\[6pt] &\text{(}dt\text{: time interval between sending the two messages; }\hat r_0\text{, }\hat r_1\text{: directions of the two }\overrightarrow{QP}\text{)}\\ &\text{(}v\text{, }a\text{: velocity and acceleration of }Q\text{ at }t_0\text{; the distance from }Q(t_0)\text{ to }Q(t_1)\text{ is }v\,dt + \tfrac12\,a\,dt^2\text{)} \end{aligned} $$

Next, by the same reasoning as in 3.1, Coulomb's Law + special relativity is enough to analyze the electric field built by an accelerating \(Q\). Only, this yields a very ugly mathematical expression with no apparent pattern, and in the end one still has to rely on numerical solutions.

Wait!? Why, when acceleration is involved, is general relativity not needed?
Because \(Q\) is a point, not a rocket with length. Using \(Q(t_i)\) at each instant, the whole process can be analyzed; the accelerating case is really no different from the constant-velocity case.

3.1, 3.2, 3.3… the whole chapter works this way and is in fact not limited to a case as narrow as constant velocity. Looking at \(q\) in the \(Q\) frame: the \({}^{Q}\vec E_{q}\) that \(q\) experiences is independent of both the velocity \({}^{Q}\vec v_{q}\) and the acceleration \({}^{Q}\vec a_{q}\) of \(q\). Conversely, looking at \(Q\) in the \(q\) frame: even if \(Q\) has an acceleration \({}^{q}\vec a_{Q}\), the analysis only needs the \({}^{q}\vec u\) at the instant \(Q(t_i)\) sends the message.

What if, instead, an entire infinite plane uniformly covered with \(Q\), originally at rest, suddenly starts simple harmonic motion (every \(Q\) moves simultaneously and identically, and the distance on the ground between two neighboring \({}^{\text{G}}Q\) always stays the same)?

$$ \begin{aligned} &{}^{q}x = \cos 2\pi f t\\[6pt] &{}^{q}u = -\,2\pi f\cdot\sin 2\pi f t\\[6pt] &\text{(amplitude taken as 1; }f\text{: frequency; origin at the center of oscillation;}\\ &\text{ }t\text{ counted from the start of oscillation; at }t = 0\text{, }Q\text{ is at rest at }{}^{q}x = 1\text{)} \end{aligned} $$

The resulting wave looks like a sin wave, but is the resulting wave really a sin wave?

First, look at \({}^{q}E_y\). If the plane moves forward at constant velocity \({}^{q}u\), there are two lines of reasoning.

Reasoning 1 (1.3): Switch to the \(Q\) frame, where the plane is at rest; then use the result of 3.1 to switch back to the \(q\) frame.

$$ \begin{aligned} &{}^{Q}E_y = \frac{{}^{Q}\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\quad\text{(Coulomb's Law summed patch by patch; the whole plane occupies half the sphere)}\\[6pt] &{}^{q}\sigma = \gamma\cdot{}^{Q}\sigma\quad\text{(spacing shortened, 1.3)}\\[6pt] &{}^{q}E_y = \gamma\cdot{}^{Q}E_y = \frac{\gamma\cdot{}^{Q}\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac{{}^{q}\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\quad\text{(perpendicular component, 3.1)}\\[6pt] &\text{(}\sigma\text{: charge per unit area of the plane; }\gamma\text{ computed with the plane's speed }{}^{q}u\text{)} \end{aligned} $$

Reasoning 2 (1.2): \({}^{Q}E_y\) and \({}^{q}\sigma\) are the same as in Reasoning 1; the relation between \({}^{q}E_y\) and \({}^{Q}E_y\) is instead seen through time dilation. For the test charge \(q\) at rest in the \(q\) frame, the change in perpendicular momentum is the same in both frames; but for the same change, the time measured in the \(Q\) frame is longer by a factor of \(\gamma\) (1.2). Force is change in momentum ÷ time, so the force measured in the \(q\) frame is larger by a factor of \(\gamma\):

$$ \begin{aligned} &d{}^{Q}p_{\perp} = d{}^{q}p_{\perp},\qquad d{}^{Q}t = \gamma\,d{}^{q}t\ \text{(1.2)}\\[6pt] &{}^{q}F_{\perp} = \frac{d{}^{q}p_{\perp}}{d{}^{q}t} = \gamma\cdot\frac{d{}^{Q}p_{\perp}}{d{}^{Q}t} = \gamma\cdot{}^{Q}F_{\perp}\\[6pt] &{}^{q}E_y = \gamma\cdot{}^{Q}E_y = \frac{\gamma\cdot{}^{Q}\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac{{}^{q}\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} \end{aligned} $$

However large the plane's speed \({}^{q}u\) is, \({}^{q}E_y\) depends only on \({}^{q}\sigma\), and \({}^{Q}E_y\) depends only on \({}^{Q}\sigma\); moreover, this holds for any acceleration, because analyzing an accelerating case only requires analyzing the velocity at that instant.
Note, however: in the plane here, every \({}^{\text{G}}Q\) moves according to the same \({}^{q}x = \cos 2\pi f t\), and the distance on the ground between two neighboring \({}^{\text{G}}Q\) never changes; so what stays constant is \({}^{q}\sigma\), and hence \({}^{q}E_y\) also stays constant. What changes with \({}^{q}u\) is \({}^{Q}\sigma = \dfrac{{}^{q}\sigma}{\gamma}\).
How can this be realized in engineering? One cannot fix \(Q\) on a rigid plate and then push the whole plate: the plate's own length stays the same, but seen from the ground the plate is shortened (1.3), so the spacing becomes smaller. Every \({}^{\text{G}}Q\) must receive, simultaneously on the ground, the same \({}^{\text{G}}\)force\((t)\); for example, free electrons in a conductor driven by an electric field \({}^{\text{G}}E(t)\) that is the same everywhere: the accelerations, velocities, and displacements of the free electrons are all the same, so no matter how the electric field strengthens over time, the distances between the free electrons stay the same. This is exactly why the wire of 3.2, when carrying a current, is still uncharged in the ground frame.

Then what about \({}^{q}E_x\)?

Focus on \(\vec P_1\). At constant velocity, \(\overrightarrow{P_2P_0}\) and the red line are left-right symmetric, and the horizontal parts cancel each other. When accelerating, the lower ends of both lines move further right than at constant velocity, by \(\tfrac12\,a\,(2dt)^2\):

$$ \begin{aligned} &\overrightarrow{P_2P_0}\text{: horizontal }-\,{}^{q}u\,(2dt) - \tfrac12\,a\,(2dt)^2,\qquad \text{vertical }c\,(2dt)\\[6pt] &\text{red line: horizontal }+\,{}^{q}u\,(2dt) - \tfrac12\,a\,(2dt)^2,\qquad \text{vertical }c\,(2dt)\\[6pt] &\text{sum: horizontal }-\,a\,(2dt)^2,\qquad \text{vertical }2\cdot c\,(2dt)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{-\,a\,(2dt)^2}{2\cdot c\,(2dt)} = -\,\frac{a\,dt}{c}\\[6pt] &\text{(}a\text{: acceleration of }Q\text{; at constant velocity }a = 0\text{, and left and right cancel completely)} \end{aligned} $$

That is, when \(dt\) is very small, the electric field near the point \(\vec P_1\) satisfies \(\dfrac{{}^{q}E_x}{{}^{q}E_y} = -\,\dfrac{a\,dt}{c}\), and \({}^{q}E_y\) is a fixed constant.

$$ \begin{aligned} &{}^{q}E_x = -\,\frac{{}^{q}E_y}{c}\int_{t=0}^{t-\frac{y}{c}} a\,dt = -\,\frac{{}^{q}E_y}{c}\int_{t=0}^{t-\frac{y}{c}} \frac{d\,{}^{q}u}{dt}\,dt\\[6pt] &\phantom{{}^{q}E_x} = -\,\frac{{}^{q}E_y}{c}\int_{t=0}^{t-\frac{y}{c}} \frac{d\big(-\,2\pi f\cdot\sin 2\pi f t\big)}{dt}\,dt\\[6pt] &\phantom{{}^{q}E_x} = -\,\frac{{}^{q}E_y}{c}\Big[-\,2\pi f\cdot\sin 2\pi f t\Big]_{t=0}^{t-\frac{y}{c}}\\[6pt] &\phantom{{}^{q}E_x} = \frac{{}^{q}E_y}{c}\Big(2\pi f\cdot\sin 2\pi f\Big(t-\frac{y}{c}\Big)\Big) \end{aligned} $$ $$ \begin{aligned} &{}^{q}E_x = \frac{{}^{q}\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{2\pi f}{c}\cdot\sin\left(2\pi f t - 2\pi\cdot\frac{y}{\lambda}\right),\qquad \lambda = \frac{c}{f}\\[6pt] &{}^{q}E_y = \frac{{}^{q}\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\ \text{(above the plane; the same at any time and any height)}\\[6pt] &\text{(}t\text{: counted from when }Q\text{ starts simple harmonic motion; }y\text{: distance to the plane, }0 < y < c\,t\text{; farther out, }{}^{q}E_x = 0\text{)} \end{aligned} $$

We have just radiated, with our bare hands, a beautiful sin electric field wave, which everyone calls an electromagnetic wave. The magnetic field has nothing whatsoever to do with any of this; the concept of "magnetism" is truly not a necessity. With sin, the most basic component, electric field waves of any waveform can be assembled. Fine! Reluctantly, let's rewrite the result in the magnetic field form that people observe:

$$ \begin{aligned} &{}^{q}\vec B = \frac{{}^{q}\vec u\times{}^{q}\vec E}{c^2}\qquad\text{(3.4; ground frame = }q\text{ frame)}\\[6pt] &\phantom{{}^{q}\vec B} = \frac{{}^{q}u\,\hat x\times\big({}^{q}E_x\,\hat x + {}^{q}E_y\,\hat y\big)}{c^2} = \frac{{}^{q}u\cdot{}^{q}E_y}{c^2}\,\hat z\\[6pt] &{}^{q}\vec B = \frac{{}^{q}E_y}{c^2}\cdot\Big(-\,2\pi f\cdot\sin 2\pi f\Big(t-\frac{y}{c}\Big)\Big)\,\hat z = -\,\frac{{}^{q}E_x}{c}\,\hat z \end{aligned} $$

3.11　Metal Shielding Is Just Economics

When analyzing metal shielding, one should not use "electric field lines," nor make a circular argument such as "electric field lines do not pass through the interior of a metal," because in the end the circular argument still cannot explain: why do electric field lines not pass through the interior of a metal? One should not even mention the "electric field"; we should go back and start over using only Coulomb's law, focusing on the force on a single particle.

\(Q\) first pulls the nearest electron to the surface (where the normal force of the metal holds the electron in place), leaving a hole at the electron's original position. A slightly more distant electron moves into the hole; on the way, the attractions of the left and right holes cancel each other, and the attractions of the upper and lower positive ions also cancel each other, so that in the end only the attraction of \(Q\) and the repulsion from the charge on the shell remain. Once the hole is filled, the hole has moved slightly farther away; an even more distant electron in turn moves into the hole, so the hole runs farther and farther away, until the hole reaches the surface far from \(Q\). As long as the attraction of \(Q\) is greater than the repulsion from the charge on the shell, a second electron is pulled over from the next lane, followed by a third and a fourth electron from the next lane, until the attraction of \(Q\) equals the repulsion from the charge on the shell. Why the next lane? Because the electrons in the next lane feel a smaller repulsion from the charge on the shell.

The shell is just the outermost layer of atoms, and even the outermost layer is mostly neutralized. There is only 1 hole for about every 92,000 surface atoms.

Once metal shielding has been thought through clearly, one finds that, seen microscopically, metal shielding is more like economics than physics. Metal shielding works on the same principle as the law of supply and demand: the attraction between \(Q\) and the electrons is their mutual demand; the repulsion from the charge on the shell is the cost of supply. Each additional electron supplied to the surface increases the repulsion from the charge on the shell a little; in other words, the local cost of living rises accordingly. As long as demand exceeds cost, the electron sea keeps supplying; once cost catches up with demand, electrons no longer show up for the rendezvous. The electron sea supplies only the exact number of electrons needed, and the remaining electrons stay where they are. Therefore, apart from the thin layer of shell, the interior of the metal is entirely electrically neutral and in static equilibrium everywhere: every electron inside the metal feels a net force of zero, which is what is commonly described as "no electric field lines pass through." Then what if we simply carve a large cavity out of the interior of the metal? No harm, right? Makes no difference, right? Nobody cares anyway.

Therefore, the interior of the metal block is shielded from the Coulomb force coming from \(Q\), that is, from what is called the electric field or electric field lines. Everything above concerns the static case, that is, the electrons move until the electrons can no longer be pulled. And magnetism is just electricity seen after changing to another frame (3.4, 3.9, 3.10); even though the electric field of an electromagnetic wave keeps changing, in the analysis we only need to look at a snapshot of all the parties involved at that instant (3.9). As long as the electron sea can keep up with the speed of \(Q\), without lagging too far behind, the electron sea can, to a certain degree, protect the fellow electrons inside the metal, safeguarding the electrical neutrality and static equilibrium of the interior electron sea; and so, in this way, the electromagnetic waves that people see are shielded. In practice, metal really does keep up from radio waves through visible light; but from ultraviolet upward, metal can no longer keep up, and the electromagnetic waves pass through the metal.
